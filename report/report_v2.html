International money and talent, 2016–26 signing classes

Where clubs spent in the international amateur market, what came out of it, and what a future big leaguer looked like at signing and in his first three years. Money buys prospect status more than big leaguers, position and size add little once you know the bonus, and by year three you can usually tell.

What we found

  • Money buys prospect status much more than it buys big leaguers. Bonus tier sorts who becomes a 50+ FV prospect very well (AUC 0.83), but sorts who reaches the majors only modestly (AUC 0.61). In the 2018–19 classes, $1M+ signees reached MLB at 20% against 2.2% for everyone else, yet 74% of the big leaguers came from the under-$300K group.
  • Cheap signings are not a proven bargain per dollar. Whether the low end returns more WAR per dollar depends entirely on what the unreported signees were paid. It holds if they averaged $10K and disappears at $50K or $100K. The published pool math implies about $94K, so the test fails.
  • Country adds nothing once you know the bonus. Adding country to a bonus-only model made out-of-sample predictions slightly worse (log-loss change -0.006, CI -0.016 to +0.003). The market prices the Dominican Republic and Venezuela about right. Output shares track money shares: the Dominican Republic had 43% of recorded money and produced 49% of the big leaguers.
  • Player type and size add little once you know the bonus. Shortstops are 13% of signees but 23% of big leaguers, and left-handers and catchers are under-represented. Bonus tier explains most of that (position test p = 0.16). At signing, big leaguers measured the same as everyone else: pitchers a median 6'2", 175 lb; hitters 6'0", 170 lb. All four size tests failed.
  • By year three you can usually tell. 92% of future big leaguers were in full-season ball by their third pro season. Of the cheap signees who weren't, 0.4% made it. That early path sorts big leaguers far better than the bonus did (AUC 0.84 vs 0.60).
  • Most of the money moves on day one, and day-one signees do better even within the same bonus tier. 68% of recorded bonus money was committed on the first day of a period. Within the same bonus tier, signees from the first two weeks reached MLB more often than later signees (odds ratio 1.65, CI 1.15–2.36). The early group is the pre-agreed, long-scouted players, so this is a quality signal and does not show that timing itself causes anything.
  • Club 'international skill' is noise over these seven classes. After adjusting for each club's bonus mix, the spread in MLB arrival rates across the 30 clubs is what random assignment produces (p = 0.36). Big club WAR totals come from one or two players each.
The market

About a thousand players sign each period, and about 1 in 30 reaches the majors within seven seasons.

The universe is every player whose first move with a big-league organization since July 2, 2016 is an international amateur signing: 9,984 players over ten signing periods. The 19 professional signings (Ohtani, Sasaki, Cubans signed at 23 or older) are held out of every test so one player can't swing a result. Recorded bonuses cover only about half the money. In the 2018-19 period, recorded bonuses totaled $70M against a league-wide pool of $158M. The gap is almost all small bonuses that public lists never report.

Bonus tier

Big bonuses hit far more often, but most big leaguers still come from the cheap end.

Signees are split by bonus at signing. A $1M+ signee from the 2018–19 classes was about nine times as likely to reach the majors as a signee under $300K or with no reported bonus. There are so few of them, though, that the cheap end still produced 74% of the big leaguers. Preregistered test T1 passed for both outcomes.

Per dollar, the answer depends on what the unreported signees were paid

Test T5 asked whether signees under $1M return more WAR per dollar than $1M+ signees, and required the answer to hold under all three assumptions about unreported bonuses. It holds at $10K each (ratio 7.2x) but not at $50K (2.1x, CI 0.8–11.2) or $100K (1.1x, CI 0.4–4.9), so it fails. The pool math for 2018-19 (added after the tests, so exploratory) puts the unreported signees at about $94K each. On that basis, the under-$300K group produced about 1.7 times as many big leaguers per $10M as the $1M+ tier. That comes from one class, and the $300K–$999K tier has only three big leaguers.

Country

Output tracks money by country. Cuban money buys fewer big leaguers, but older and quicker ones.

Each country's share of recorded bonus money is set against its share of the big leaguers, for the 2016–22 classes. Every big leaguer and every 50+ FV prospect in the study has a known country. The unrecorded small money most likely went mainly to Dominican and Venezuelan players, since they fill most DSL rosters. If so, their true money shares sit a little above what is shown.

Test T2 asked whether country helps predict a 50+ FV prospect once the bonus is known. It does not. Adding country made leave-one-class-out predictions slightly worse (log loss 0.370 with bonus alone, 0.377 with country), so the test fails. The test group (277 signees with a recorded bonus) leans to the top of the market. Cuba's pattern comes from age. Cuban $1M+ signees were about 19 at signing, against about 17 for Dominican and Venezuelan ones. Half of them reached MLB, against about a fifth of the Dominican and Venezuelan $1M+ signees, and Cuban big leaguers debuted a median 5 seasons after signing, against 6.5 for Dominicans and 6 for Venezuelans.

The 2016-17 period was the last before hard pool caps. Clubs that went over paid a tax instead of being blocked, and Luis Robert's $26M bonus alone is about a quarter of that year's recorded money. The 2020 period never happened: it moved to January 2021.

Market timing

The market for top talent clears on day one.

68% of recorded bonus money was committed on the first day of a signing period, and 83% of $1M+ bonuses were signed in the first two weeks. After that the market is volume: most of the 6,029 signings in the 2016–22 classes came months into a period.

Test T3 compared signees from the first two weeks with later signees, controlling for bonus tier and class. Early signees reached the majors more often (odds ratio 1.65, CI 1.15–2.36) and became 50+ FV prospects more often (odds ratio 2.26, CI 1.18–4.33). Both results pass and survive the multiple-test correction. The gap comes from the cheap end: early signees under $300K reached MLB at 3.9%, against 1.9% for later ones. Among $1M+ signees the direction flips, because the late big bonuses are mostly older Cubans who were cleared to sign mid-period and arrive quickly.

How to read the timing result: a day-one signing is usually a deal agreed years in advance on a player the club has scouted since he was 13 or 14. Later signings include players found late, players whose deals fell through, and the depth that fills out a DSL roster. The gap shows how much information clubs act on early. It does not show that waiting costs anything, and the later group is cheaper per player. The open question is whether the later market has pockets where clubs underprice players; answering it needs signing dates and bonuses for every player.

Coverage

Pre-signing top-30 lists find half the top prospects but fewer than one in five big leaguers.

Players on a public pre-signing ranking (the top 30, or top 50 from 2022) are 3.5% of signees. They reached MLB at 20%, against 2.9% for unranked signees. Because unranked players outnumber them about 28 to 1, unranked players still produced most of the big leaguers.

Player types

Shortstops are over-represented among big leaguers, but mostly because they get the money.

Position at signing comes from the signing transaction and is known for 95% of signees. Shortstops reach the majors at the highest rate. Catchers and left-handers reach at about a third of that rate. The share chart sets each position's share of all 2016–19 signees against its share of the 143 big leaguers.

2016–19 classes
Show the numbers

Test Q1 asked whether position still matters once bonus tier and class are known. It fails for reaching MLB (p = 0.16) and for becoming a 50+ FV prospect (p = 0.10). Shortstops look good mostly because clubs pay them. One contrast is worth watching but is not proven: once the bonus is known, catchers were about 3.6 times as likely as right-handed pitchers to become 50+ FV prospects, yet they reached MLB at only 2.0%. Prospect grades may like young catchers more than the position ends up rewarding.

Hitters make up about half the big leaguers and most of the value

Hitters were 47% of 2016–19 signees and reached MLB at 3.9%, against 3.2% for pitchers. They produced 71% of the first-seven-season WAR (101 vs 42). Left-handers were 19% of the pitchers signed but 12% of the pitchers who made it.

Position at signing is a starting point, not a destination. Of the 42 big leaguers from the 2016–22 classes who signed as shortstops, 14 played mostly shortstop in the majors. 12 moved to second and 9 to third. Catchers stayed put: 16 of 19. Hitter-to-pitcher conversions were rare; the clearest one is Daysbel Hernández, who signed as an outfielder and pitched in the majors.

Size and age

At signing, future big leaguers were built like everyone else.

Height and weight near signing exist only for players on a public list within two seasons of signing. That is 7.4% of 2016–19 signees, but 52% of the eventual big leaguers. Within that group, big leaguers matched everyone else. Pitchers who made it had a median of 6'2", 175 lb (middle half 6'0"–6'3"), the same as all listed pitchers. Hitters who made it had a median of 6'0", 170 lb, against 6'0", 175 lb for all listed hitters.

Test Q2 asked whether height and weight help once the bonus is known, separately for pitchers and hitters and for both outcomes. All four fail, and none comes close. One pattern is worth noting (exploratory, small samples): tall hitters got the prospect grades but not the big-league careers. Hitters 6'3" or taller became 50+ FV prospects at 27%, against 7% for those 5'10" or shorter. They reached MLB at 17%, against 30%.

Age at signing doesn't separate them either. Test Q4 compared signing at 17 or older with signing at 16, after bonus tier, among listed signees. The odds ratio was 0.76 (CI 0.43–1.35), so the test fails. The big leaguers debuted at a median age of 22.5, and most debuted between 21 and 24.

A first run of the age test used every known birth date. Birth dates exist for every big leaguer but only 12% of everyone else, so that cohort was selected on the outcome. It was discarded and rerun on listed players (see PREREG_v2_AMENDMENTS.md). Its result was also null.

The first three years

Year three is where the picture comes into focus.

Each signee's first seasons are traced through his assignments (2020 skipped). This is where future big leaguers separate from the field. Nearly every one of them was still with a club after two seasons, out of the DSL by his second season, and in full-season ball by his third. Each step cuts the field sharply but keeps almost all of the big leaguers.

Test Q3 asked whether 'in full-season ball by year three' sorts future big leaguers better than the bonus did at signing. It does: AUC 0.84 against 0.60, a gap of 0.23 (CI 0.19–0.27). The test passes and survives correction. The miss rate for cheap signees is the useful number: of the 3,032 under-$300K signees who weren't in full-season ball by year three, 11 reached MLB (0.4%). For those who were, the rate was 9.9%.

The exceptions are real, but few. 11 of the 143 big leaguers were not in full-season ball by year three: Maikel Garcia and Yainer Díaz are the best of them. 3 were released before they debuted. 48 of the 143 (34%) were traded before their debut, so a third of these big leaguers debuted for a club that did not sign them.

What this means for coverage: the signing-day decision is a low-information bet, about 3% in aggregate. Within three years the picture sharpens a lot, which makes the second and third pro seasons a natural point to re-evaluate, trade for, or protect players. A club that tracks other organizations' DSL and complex-league players, not only its own, sees that signal early too.

Clubs

Club differences in turning signees into big leaguers look like luck.

For each club, every 2016–19 signee gets the arrival rate of his class and bonus tier. Summing those gives the club's expected number of big leaguers, which is compared with how many it actually got. Test T4 shuffled clubs 2,000 times within class and tier. The real spread across clubs was no wider than the shuffled spreads (p = 0.36), so the test fails. Shrunk toward the league, every club lands between 0.96 and 1.04 times expected.

Method and sources

  • Signings: These come from the 2016–26 transaction log. A signing is a player's first move with any big-league organization, either a signing or a first assignment to a Dominican or Venezuelan Summer League club, from July 2, 2016 on. The player also needs an international marker: a DSL or VSL assignment within 36 months, an international signing market on a public prospect list, or a known birth country outside the US, Puerto Rico and Canada with age under 25. Drafted players are dropped. Periods run July 2 to July 1 through 2018-19. The 2019-20 period runs to January 14, 2021. From 2021 on, periods start January 15.
  • Bonuses: These come from public prospect lists (FanGraphs) and only exist for players those lists covered. Tiers use bonuses recorded within two seasons of signing. Pipeline-ranked players with no recorded bonus get the median for their rank band (97 players). For the 2016-17 periods, only $1M+ bonuses are identifiable, so those classes use tier A versus everyone else. League pool total for 2018-19 (MLB Trade Rumors, April 2018): $158.4M.
  • Outcomes: MLB arrival comes from the org spine and house WAR (2021+) by player id, plus pitcher debut dates. 2016–20 public season lines are joined by name and season, unique matches only, and only for players with id-based MLB evidence. FV 50+ is a FanGraphs FV of 50 or higher on a list published one to four seasons after signing. Top 100 is a FanGraphs Top 100 from 2019 on, or an MLB Pipeline Top 100. 'Ranked' means a Pipeline international top 30/50 or a FanGraphs international list in the signing year.
  • Preregistration: PREREG_v1.md was sealed before outcomes were joined. Two coverage fixes (pre-2021 MLB seasons; Top 100 list rule) were made before any test ran and are recorded in PREREG_v1_AMENDMENTS.md. Three views were added after the tests and are labeled exploratory: the pool-based per-dollar view; the country ages and years to debut; and a timing refit on the 96% of signings with a logged date (MLB odds ratio 1.54, CI 1.07–2.22; FV 50+ odds ratio 2.15, CI 1.13–4.10), which tells the same story. Benjamini–Hochberg at 0.10 over six p-values: T1 (both) and T3 (both) survive; T2 and T4 do not.
  • Part 2 (player types, sizes, early paths): PREREG_v2.md was sealed before any split by position, size, age or path. Position at signing comes from the signing transaction text. Size comes from the earliest public list within two seasons of signing. Pro-season levels come from assignments mapped to team levels. 'Full-season' means Low-A or higher. Q1 (position) and Q2 (size, four sub-tests) fail; Q3 (year-three path vs bonus) passes; Q4 (age, rerun on listed players after a cohort flaw, see PREREG_v2_AMENDMENTS.md) fails. Benjamini–Hochberg at 0.10 across the eight: only Q3 survives.
  • Limits: Country is known for every big leaguer but only for about 14% of all signees, so country rates per signee can't be computed. Signing club gets the credit even after a trade. Classes from 2022 on are too young for MLB outcomes. No new data was pulled for this study.
